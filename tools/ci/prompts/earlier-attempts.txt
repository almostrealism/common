## Find out what has already been tried, and what was refused

You are probably not the first session sent after this failure. A failure
stays on the list until a fix is merged, so every round is handed it again, and
an earlier round's fix may be sitting in an open pull request, or may have been
turned down by the owner. A fix the owner has turned down is not available to
you, however reasonable it looks when you arrive at it yourself.

Before you change any code:

1. List the open pull requests with `github_list_open_prs`. For each one whose
   title or branch could concern the same test, component or error, read its
   inline comments with `github_pr_review_comments` (pass
   `include_resolved=true`) and its discussion with `github_pr_conversation`.
2. Search the tracker with `tracker_search_tasks`, and memories with
   `memory_recall` using `scope="repo"`, for the failing test's name, the
   component in the stack trace and the words of the error message.
3. Write down, in your summary, each earlier attempt you found: the pull
   request, what it changed, and what the owner said about it.

Then hold your own fix to what you found:

- **An approach the owner rejected is rejected for you too.** Do not submit it
  again under another name or in another file. If the owner said where the
  real defect is, that is the defect to fix. If fixing it is more than this
  round can do, say so and file it; do not substitute the rejected fix.
- **An open pull request that already makes the right fix means this failure
  is being handled.** Do not open a second one. Report the pull request and
  move to the next failure.

## Fix the cause, not the place the failure surfaced

The error names where something finally broke: a compiler rejecting generated
source, a limit exceeded, a timeout, an allocation refused. That is rarely
where the defect is. Ask what produced the thing that broke, and why it has
the shape it has.

Changing how a result is written out so that it no longer shows the problem,
or raising the limit, the timeout or the tolerance that reported it, makes the
test pass and leaves the defect in place. Generated source that is too large or
too deeply nested, for example, means the expression it was generated from was
built or simplified wrongly; the fix is in that expression, not in the code
that prints it and not in the compiler's settings. A fix of that concealing
kind is worse than no fix, because it removes the only signal that the defect
exists.
