## Where you are running, and why it matters

This job is routed to a macOS node so that Metal is available. That is
the point of the exercise: the framework compiles computation graphs to
native kernels, and the circumstances that matter most are the ones where
a GPU is present. An optimization that looks good on the CPU backend and
is neutral or worse on Metal is a regression for the case we care about.

So the ground rules for every measurement you take:

- Confirm at the start that you actually have Metal. Run something small
  and check the backend it reports before you trust any timing. If Metal
  is not available, say so, report that the round could not be carried
  out under the intended conditions, and stop — do not fall back to
  optimizing for the CPU and presenting the result as though it were the
  GPU result.
- Measure on Metal (`AR_HARDWARE_DRIVER=mtl`, passed through the test
  runner's `jvm_args` as `-DAR_HARDWARE_DRIVER=mtl`), and measure the
  same test on the native CPU backend (`-DAR_HARDWARE_DRIVER=native`) as
  well. A change is only acceptable if Metal is faster and native is not
  meaningfully slower. Both numbers go in your report.
- Timing is noisy. Use the test runner's `repetitions` option and
  `get_run_timing` to get more than one sample, and compare distributions
  rather than single runs. Say how many repetitions you took.

## How to go about it

**1. Find a test that should be faster than it is.**

Every test in this repository carries a `timeout` on its `@Test`
annotation. Those values are a rough map of where the time goes: a test
with a large timeout was given one because it was slow when it was
written. Use them to build a shortlist, then check the shortlist against
reality by actually running the candidates — the annotation records what
someone once expected, and expectation and fact drift apart. Where a
test's real duration is wildly out of line with its timeout in either
direction — a ten-minute allowance on a test that finishes in seconds,
or a test that routinely runs close to its limit — call that out in your
summary. Those deviations are useful in their own right, whether or not
you end up working on that test.

Prefer a test whose time is spent in the framework rather than in test
scaffolding: a test that compiles and runs a large computation graph is
a candidate; a test that is slow because it writes a big file is not
interesting here. Prefer a test whose slowness reflects something many
callers pay for, so that the improvement is worth more than the one
test. `ar-profile-analyzer` is the primary tool for this: run the
candidate so an operation profile is produced, load it, and use
`get_timing_breakdown` and `find_slowest` to see which operations the
time actually goes to, and `get_source` to see what the compiled kernel
does. Do not guess at where the time goes from reading Java; the
profile is the evidence.

Say which test you chose, what its timeout is, what it actually takes on
Metal and on native, and where the profile says the time goes, before
you change anything.

**2. Form a plan before you edit.**

Write down what you intend to change and why you expect it to be
faster: which operation, what about its compiled form or its scheduling
is wasteful, and what the improved version would do instead. Consider
more than one approach, and prefer the one that improves the framework
over the one that special-cases this test's shape. A plan that only
makes this one test fast is worth less than one that makes a class of
computations faster.

Keep the fundamental rule in view: Java is orchestration, not execution.
The computation is described as `Producer` compositions and compiled to
native code, and that is where the time goes. The improvements that
count are ones that change what gets compiled or how it is scheduled —
a fused kernel, an avoided copy, a redundant compilation cached, a
better traversal — not ones that move work into Java loops, which is
the single worst thing you could do here.

**3. Make the change and measure it.**

Take your baseline measurements first, on both backends, with enough
repetitions to be meaningful. Then make the change. Then measure again
under exactly the same conditions. Report both sets of numbers, and the
profile after the change, so that a reader can see that the operation
you targeted is the one that got cheaper.

"Meaningfully faster" means a reduction that is clearly outside the
noise of your repeated runs, on Metal, and that you can attribute to
the change. A five percent improvement that is inside run-to-run
variance is not a result. If the change does not produce a measurable
improvement, say so, revert it, and either try the next approach in
your plan or report honestly that the target did not yield.

**4. Do not break anything.**

The test you chose must still pass, with its assertions unchanged. The
tests around the code you touched must still pass. Run them and report
the output. If a test elsewhere starts failing because of your change,
that is a defect in the change, not an obstacle to remove.

## What counts as success

In descending order:

1. A measured, reproducible reduction in the test's time on Metal, with
   native no worse, from a change to the framework, with the tests
   around it still passing.
2. A careful account of which test you chose, what the profile showed,
   what you tried, and why it did not yield — plus any timeout
   annotations you found to be badly out of line with reality.

The second outcome is a real result and you should be willing to return
it. Reporting a speed-up you did not measure, or one measured only on
the CPU, is worse than an honest empty-handed round.

## Constraints

- Do not edit any existing test method. Not its timeout, not its
  dimensions, not its assertions, not its `@TestDepth`. Making a test
  faster by making it do less is the thing this round exists to avoid,
  and it is detected mechanically. If you believe a timeout annotation is
  wrong, say so in your summary; changing it is not yours to do.
- Do not move computation out of `Producer` compositions into Java. Do
  not call `.evaluate()`, `.toDouble()` or friends inside computation
  code. Any change that does this is a regression regardless of what the
  stopwatch says on the CPU.
- Report what you ran and what it printed. "It felt faster" and
  "verified locally" are not evidence; the timing output on this machine,
  on both backends, is.
- Keep the change focused on one improvement. If the profile shows
  several opportunities, take the most valuable and note the others in
  your summary.
